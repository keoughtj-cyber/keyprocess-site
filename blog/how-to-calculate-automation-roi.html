How to calculate automation ROI (and know if it's worth it before you spend a dollar)

The formula, a five-step method a busy owner can finish in ten minutes, worked examples, real results, and the costs most ROI math leaves out.

By Tyler, Founder of KeyProcess Automation Services · Published November 2, 2026 · 10 minute read

The short answer: to calculate automation ROI, take the yearly value of the time and mistakes it saves, subtract what the automation costs in a year, divide by that cost, and multiply by 100. For a quicker gut check, divide the upfront price by the monthly savings to see how many months it takes to pay for itself.

Key takeaways

  • Automation ROI = (yearly value − yearly cost) ÷ yearly cost × 100. Payback in months = upfront cost ÷ monthly savings.
  • Value an hour at what it really costs you, not just the wage. Benefits add about 30% on average.
  • Count only the share of a task automation actually takes over. It's rarely 100%.
  • A good first project pays for itself in months, not years. If payback is longer than a year, pick a different task.
  • Ongoing care pays off best across several automations, not on one small one.

What is the automation ROI formula?

Automation ROI is the yearly value an automation creates, minus what it costs, as a percentage of the cost.

ROI (%) = (yearly value − yearly cost) ÷ yearly cost × 100

Two more numbers are worth working out, because owners usually care about them more than a percentage:

  • Payback period (months) = upfront cost ÷ monthly savings. This is how long until the automation has paid for itself. After that, the savings are yours.
  • Yearly net savings = yearly value − yearly cost. This is the actual dollars back in your pocket each year.

Most guides stop at the formula. The hard part is getting honest inputs, which is what the next section is for.

How do you calculate automation ROI, step by step?

Work it out one task at a time, in five steps. Use real numbers from your week, not hopes.

1. Time the task honestly

Write down how long the task takes each time and how often it happens. "Building the Monday report takes about 90 minutes" or "each new client takes 40 minutes of paperwork, about 12 a month." Include the small stuff people forget: finding the file, chasing a missing number, fixing a typo, and re-sending. If you aren't sure, time it for one week. Guesses are almost always too low.

2. Put a real dollar figure on an hour

Use what an hour really costs you, not just the hourly wage. Employers paid an average of $46.89 per hour worked for private industry workers in June 2026, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Of that, $32.82 was wages and $14.07 (30%) was benefits.

Admin work usually pays less than that average. The median pay for general office clerks was $21.64 an hour in May 2025, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. If benefits make up the same 30% share, that person costs you about $31 an hour. If the task lands on you, the owner, use a higher number. Your hour is worth what you could be doing with it instead.

3. Estimate how much of the task automation takes over

Automation rarely removes 100% of a task. Someone still glances at the report, approves the message, or handles the odd exception. A fair starting assumption is 70% to 90% of the time for a repetitive, rule-based task, and less for anything that needs judgment. When we quote a project, we tell you what will still need a person.

4. Add up every cost

Include the build price, any monthly upkeep, and a few hours of your own time for the audit, answering questions, and testing. With us, one automated process starts at $1,000, a package of 3 to 5 connected automations starts at $3,000, and Automation Care, which keeps everything monitored, fixed, and improved, starts at $500 a month. Our guide to what business automation costs explains what moves a price up or down.

5. Run the numbers

Weekly hours saved × your hourly cost × 52 = yearly value. Then plug that into the formulas above. If the answer is close, do the math again with more cautious inputs. A project that only works on optimistic numbers isn't a good first project.

A worked example

An example, with made-up numbers. An office manager spends 5 hours a week building a weekly client report, at about $31 an hour. Automation takes over 80% of it, so they get 4 hours a week back.

  • Weekly value: 4 hours × $31 = $124 a week.
  • Yearly value: $124 × 52 = $6,448.
  • Cost: a $1,000 build.
  • Payback: $1,000 ÷ $124 = about 8 weeks.
  • First-year ROI: ($6,448 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 × 100 = about 545%.

Now add $500 a month of ongoing care to that one small automation. The yearly cost becomes $7,000, and the first-year ROI drops to about −8%. That's why we don't recommend paying for care on a single small automation. Care makes sense once several automations are doing real work for you.

A second example, also with made-up numbers. A package of connected automations saves a team 15 hours a week at about $31 an hour. That's $465 a week, or $24,180 a year. With a $3,000 build and $500 a month of care, the first-year cost is $9,000 and the first-year ROI is about 169%. After care, it saves about $1,515 a month, so the build pays for itself in about 2 months.

What about the benefits that are hard to price?

Some of the best results don't show up as hours. Follow-ups stop slipping, bills get checked before they're late, and work keeps moving when a key person is out. Count these only when you can put an honest price on a miss: a late fee you stopped paying, a lead that used to go cold, or a vendor you used to chase. If you can't price it, leave it out of the ROI and treat it as a bonus. That way your numbers hold up.

What costs does ROI math usually miss?

  • Your team's time during setup. Plan on a few hours for the audit, answering questions, and testing before anything goes live.
  • New software fees. Some approaches mean paying for extra software every month. We build around the tools you already use, so most projects add nothing new, but always ask.
  • Upkeep when things change. Software updates, a new form, or a changed report layout can break an automation. Someone has to notice and fix it. That's what ongoing care covers, or what you'll do yourself.
  • The process changing under you. Automating a process you're about to redo means paying twice. Settle the process first.
  • Review time that never goes away. If someone still checks every output, count that time against the savings.

Costs are worth watching closely right now. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, 77% of employer firms said they faced challenges from rising costs in the prior 12 months (2026 Report on Employer Firms). Hiring and keeping qualified staff was among the most common operational challenges. When every hire costs more, getting hours back from the team you have is often the cheaper move.

What is a good ROI for automation?

For a small business, judge by the payback period first. Here's the rule of thumb we use:

  • Under 6 months: a strong first project. Do it.
  • 6 to 12 months: worth doing if the task is also error-prone or slows other people down.
  • Over 12 months: pick a different task first, unless there's a cost you can't put in the math, like a compliance deadline.

Automation usually doesn't pay off for tasks you do a few times a year, tasks that take a couple of minutes a week, work that needs judgment every single time, or a process that's still changing. A checklist is cheaper for those. If you're a U.S. business, some automation projects may also qualify for the federal R&D tax credit, which can lower the real cost. FAQ

Common questions about automation ROI

How do you calculate ROI on automation?

Work out the yearly value of the time and mistakes the automation saves, subtract what it costs in a year, divide by that cost, and multiply by 100. Value time at what an hour really costs you, including benefits, and count only the share of the task automation actually takes over.

How long does it take for automation to pay for itself?

Divide the upfront cost by the monthly savings. A good first project for a small business usually pays for itself within a few months. If the math says more than a year, start with a different task.

What is a good ROI for business automation?

Judge by payback first. Under 6 months is a strong first project, 6 to 12 months is worth it if the task is also error-prone, and over 12 months usually means a different task should go first.

How do I value an hour of my team's time?

Start with the hourly wage and add benefits. Benefits made up about 30% of employer costs for private industry workers in June 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, so dividing the wage by 0.7 gives a fair estimate.

Should I include ongoing maintenance in the ROI?

Yes. Include any monthly care or software fees in the yearly cost. Ongoing care usually pays off best when it covers several automations, not one small one.

Is automation ROI only about time saved?

No, but time saved is the part you can measure most reliably. Fewer missed follow-ups, avoided late fees, and work that keeps moving when someone is out are real benefits too. Count them only when you can put an honest price on a miss.
