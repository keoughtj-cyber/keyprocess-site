What is an automation audit?

An automation audit is a structured look at where your team's hours go, with one question in mind: which of these tasks could run on their own?

It usually starts with a conversation, not a software review. You describe your week: the follow-ups, the reports, the paperwork, the data that gets typed from one place into another. The person running the audit listens for work that repeats, follows the same rules every time, and costs real hours or real money when it slips.

It's sometimes called a business process audit or an automation assessment. The name matters less than the output. You should finish with a clear, ranked answer to "what should we automate first, and is it worth it?"

Why bother now? Costs are squeezing small businesses. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, 77% of employer firms said they faced challenges from rising costs in the prior 12 months, according to the 2026 Report on Employer Firms. When hiring another person is hard to justify, getting hours back from the team you already have is the next best move, and an audit tells you where those hours are.

What should an automation audit include?

A good automation audit gives you six things, ideally in writing:

  1. A list of your repetitive tasks. Every task that happens weekly or more and follows the same steps, with who does it today.
  2. Hours for each one. An honest estimate of how long each task takes per week, including the chasing, fixing, and re-sending people forget to count.
  3. A ranking. Which task to automate first, second, and third, based on hours saved, how often it goes wrong, and how simple it is to hand off.
  4. A rough cost and payback. What automating the top tasks would cost and how many months until the time saved covers it.
  5. A "leave it alone" list. The tasks that need judgment, happen too rarely, or would cost more to automate than they save. A good audit tells you what not to do.
  6. Where a person stays involved. For each automation, which step still needs someone's approval, such as anything customer-facing or involving money.

It should not require you to buy new software, switch systems, or hand over passwords before you've even agreed to anything. And it should be about outcomes ("this gives your office manager back about 4 hours a week"), not jargon.

How do you run your own automation audit?

You can run a basic automation audit yourself in under an hour. Here's the method we'd suggest.

1. Log one normal week

Ask everyone on the team to jot down the repetitive tasks they do, how often, and roughly how long each takes. Count the small stuff: finding the file, chasing a reply, and fixing a typo. A real week beats a memory.

2. Score each task

Give each task a score from 0 to 2 on four questions, then add them up:

Question012
How often does it happen?Monthly or lessWeeklyDaily
How many hours a week does it take?Under 11 to 3More than 3
Does it follow the same steps every time?RarelyMostlyAlways
What does a miss cost?Nothing muchAnnoyance or reworkLost money or customers

Tasks scoring 6 or more are strong candidates. Anything that scores 0 on "same steps every time" goes on the leave-it-alone list, however many hours it takes. If you need ideas for what belongs on the list, our guide to business processes to automate covers 15 common ones.

3. Put a dollar figure on the top three

Multiply the weekly hours by what an hour really costs you. The median pay for general office clerks was $21.64 an hour in May 2025, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and benefits made up about 30% of private employers' compensation costs in June 2026, also per the BLS. Together that's about $31 an hour. If the task lands on you, the owner, use a higher number. Our guide to calculating automation ROI walks through the full math, and the savings calculator does it for you.

4. Decide where a person stays involved

For each top task, write down which step still needs someone: approving a payment, checking a document before it goes to a client, or answering a reply. This keeps the automation safe and makes the next conversation with any provider much faster.

What a do-it-yourself audit can't easily tell you is what each automation would cost to build, what it would take with the software you already have, and which hidden steps will cause trouble. That's where an outside audit earns its keep.

What are the red flags in an automation audit?

Most red flags come down to an audit that's really a sales pitch. Watch for these:

  • Nothing in writing. If you leave with a good feeling and no list, you didn't get an audit.
  • No numbers. "This will save you a lot of time" isn't an estimate. Ask for hours per task.
  • A push to switch software. Most small businesses can automate around the tools they already use. Be wary if the first recommendation is a new subscription.
  • "Automate everything." A good auditor tells you what isn't worth it. If nothing lands on the leave-it-alone list, they weren't really looking.
  • No talk of what happens when something breaks. Automations need looking after. Ask who keeps them running, and what that costs.
  • No clear answer on ownership. You should own your accounts and your data, whoever builds the automations.
