How much does business automation cost? A plain-English guide for 2026

Real price ranges, what you are actually paying for, and a five-minute way to tell whether automating a task will pay for itself.

By KeyProcess Automation Services · Published October 19, 2026 · 8 minute read

The short answer: for most small and mid-sized businesses, automating one process starts around $1,000 as a fixed-price project, and a set of connected automations across a department starts around $3,000. Ongoing care, if you want someone watching and improving everything, typically starts around $500 a month. Whether that is expensive depends entirely on how many hours the task eats today, which you can work out below.

Ask five people what business automation costs and you will get five answers, from "it's free, just use an app" to six-figure enterprise quotes. Both can be true, because "automation" covers everything from a single email reminder to rebuilding how a whole company runs. This guide is about the middle ground most owners actually need: taking the repetitive work your team does every week, such as paperwork, follow-ups, data entry, and reports, and making it run on its own.

What you are actually paying for

Every automation project has three parts, and it helps to price them separately.

  • The build. Someone maps out how the task is done today, designs the automated version, builds it, tests it against real examples, and trains your team on what changes. This is usually a one-time cost and the biggest number on the quote.
  • The upkeep. Businesses change. A form gets a new field, a vendor changes the format of their invoices, or you add a location. Automations need someone to keep them running when that happens. You can handle this yourself, pay as you go, or pay a monthly care fee.
  • The software. Some automations need a subscription to run. For a small business this is usually a modest monthly cost, and in many cases the tools you already pay for can do more than you think.

When you compare quotes, make sure you are comparing all three. A cheap build with no upkeep plan can end up costing more than a fair build with support, because the first time something breaks, nobody is on the hook to fix it.

What makes a project cost more or less

Two projects that sound the same can be priced very differently. These are the things that move the number.

1. How many steps and decisions

"Send a reminder when an invoice is overdue" is one step. "Send a reminder, wait a week, escalate to a manager if there is no reply, and log the outcome for the monthly report" is several. Each decision point adds design and testing time.

2. How many systems are involved

A task that lives in one place is simpler than one that pulls from your inbox, a spreadsheet, and your accounting software at the same time. The more places information has to move between, the more work it is to build, and the more there is to watch afterward.

3. How many exceptions there are

The happy path is usually easy. The cost is in the exceptions: the customer with two accounts, the bill that arrives late, or the order that ships in three parts. Being honest about exceptions up front is the single best way to get an accurate quote.

4. How clean the information is

If the same thing is spelled four different ways across your records, part of the project is cleaning that up or teaching the automation to cope with it. Consistent records make for cheaper builds.

5. How much a mistake would cost

An automation that posts to social media can afford the occasional typo. One that prepares contracts or moves money needs review steps, alerts, and more testing. That extra care is worth paying for, and it shows up in the price.

How to tell if it is worth it

You do not need a spreadsheet model. You need three numbers:

  1. Hours per week the task takes today, across everyone who touches it.
  2. Cost per hour of the people doing it. Use what you actually pay, plus a bit for taxes and benefits.
  3. How much of the task can be automated. Most tasks will not disappear entirely, because someone still reviews or approves. A realistic share is often well over half.

Multiply them and you get what the task costs you each week. Divide the project price by that number and you get the number of weeks until it pays for itself.

An example, with made-up numbers. Say your office manager spends 5 hours a week building a client report, and their time costs you $25 an hour. If automation handles 80% of that, you save 4 hours a week, or $100. A $1,000 project pays for itself in 10 weeks, and every week after that is money and time back. Over a year, that is more than $5,000 saved on a single task.

Real numbers can be even more lopsided. In one of our case studies, preparing an approval packet went from up to 2 hours to about 10 minutes. Across 300 to 400 approvals a year, that adds up to roughly 55 workdays of staff time saved every year from one process.

There is also a cost that never shows up on a timesheet: mistakes. Retyped information leads to typos, missed follow-ups lead to lost revenue, and late reports lead to bad decisions. You do not need to put a number on these, but it is worth remembering that the time saved is usually the smallest part of the benefit.

Ongoing and hidden costs

A good quote spells these out. If yours does not, ask.

  • Software subscriptions. If the automation needs a paid tool, it should run in an account your business owns, billed to you directly. That way you see the real cost, and you keep everything if you ever change providers.
  • Changes in your business. New services, new staff, new forms, and new rules all ripple into your automations. Budget for small updates a few times a year, or choose a care plan that covers them.
  • Your team's time. Someone has to explain how the task works today and review the first few runs. Plan for a few hours spread over the project, not a full-time commitment.
  • The cost of a cheap build. An automation that fails silently is worse than no automation, because everyone assumes the work is getting done. Ask how you will know if something stops working.

Automation vs. hiring

Owners often frame this as "automation or another hire," but the two do different jobs. People are great at judgment, relationships, and the unexpected. Automation is great at doing the same thing correctly at 2 a.m. on a holiday, every time.

The best outcome is usually both: automation takes the repetitive, rule-based part of the job off your team's plate, so the people you already have spend their time on work that grows the business. For a lot of growing companies, that means the extra administrative hire can wait, or is not needed at all. Unlike a hire, a finished automation does not need training, does not take vacation, and does not get slower when the workload doubles.

How to keep the cost down

  • Start with one process. Pick the task your team complains about most. A small first win proves the value and teaches you what to automate next.
  • Pick high-volume, rule-based work. The more often a task repeats, and the clearer its rules, the faster it pays back. Our list of tasks you can automate is a good place to look for ideas, even outside property management.
  • Write down the steps. Even a rough list of how the task is done today, including the exceptions, saves time in the build.
  • Keep your current software. Building around the tools you already use is almost always cheaper than switching systems.
  • Insist on a fixed quote. It protects your budget and forces everyone to agree on exactly what "done" means.

Questions to ask before you hire anyone

  1. Is the price fixed, and what exactly is included?
  2. Who owns the accounts and the automations when the project is finished?
  3. What happens when something breaks or my business changes?
  4. How will I know if an automation stops working?
  5. Will my team need to learn new software?
  6. Is there a guarantee if it does not save us time?
  7. Can you show me results from work you have already done?

FAQ

Common questions about automation costs

How much does it cost to automate one business process?

For a small or mid-sized business, automating one well-defined process, such as an approval, a weekly follow-up, or a recurring report, usually starts around $1,000 as a fixed-price project. The price rises with the number of steps, systems, and exceptions involved.

Are there monthly costs after an automation is built?

Sometimes. If the automation needs a software subscription, that cost is usually small and should be billed to an account your business owns. Many businesses also pay for ongoing care, which covers monitoring, fixes, and improvements. Our Automation Care plan starts at $500 a month.

How long does it take for automation to pay for itself?

Divide the project price by the value of the time it saves each week. A task that eats several staff hours a week often pays back a single-process build within a few months, and sometimes within weeks.

Should I pay hourly or a fixed price for automation work?

A fixed price, agreed after someone has looked at your process, protects you from open-ended bills. Hourly billing makes sense only for small, open-ended tweaks.

Is automation cheaper than hiring someone?

They solve different problems. Automation takes over the repetitive, rule-based part of a job so the people you already have can focus on work that needs judgment. For many growing businesses, that delays or replaces the need for an extra administrative hire.

Do I have to change my software to automate?

No. Good automations are built around the software and email your team already uses, which also keeps the cost down.
